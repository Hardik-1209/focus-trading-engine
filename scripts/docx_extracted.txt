1. Executive finding
The bot is not failing because it lacks an LLM. The 24-hour audit shows a negative gross trading edge, severe turnover/fee drag, stops that are too tight for the traded instruments, repeated re-entry into the same volatile symbols, and an LLM approval layer that is not selective. The correct objective is therefore not “increase AI accuracy” first; it is to rebuild the pipeline so that a forecast is converted into a trade only when expected edge exceeds all-in trading friction with a meaningful margin.
The report records 202 simulated closed positions, a 37.62% win rate, -1.5015 USDT net realized PnL, 1.2119 USDT fees, -0.2896 USDT gross trading PnL, and 0.78 profit factor. Average hold duration was 1.1 minutes. The report attributes 80.7% of total loss to fees and identifies LSKUSDT and CVCUSDT as responsible for 93.7% of net losses. [PDF pp.1–2]
My central conclusion: do not add a larger neural network or more LLM calls yet. First make the system economically selective, regime-aware, cost-aware, and statistically testable. Recent 2026 research reaches a similar conclusion: ML forecasts can look useful but become unprofitable after realistic transaction costs unless the system trades only when forecast magnitude clears a cost threshold. [Research 2]
2. Complete audit of the existing bot
2.1 Current architecture reconstructed from the PDF
The system scans Bitget USDT perpetuals every 30 minutes, ranks contracts with a volatility score based on absolute 24-hour price move multiplied by log10(volume), selects the highest-volatility coin, and avoids selecting the same symbol in consecutive cycles. It then bootstraps 60 one-minute and 40 five-minute candles, maintains a live WebSocket feed, calculates EMA 9/21/50, RSI 14, ADX 14, CHOP, VWAP, MACD, ATR and volume z-score, and applies a three-tier signal hierarchy. Tier 1 is a pullback setup, Tier 2 is momentum breakout, and Tier 3 is a choppy/consolidating WAIT state. [PDF p.2]
Every trade in the reported window was Tier 2 and passed the Groq LLM. The LLM layer used a seven-key Groq array and openai/gpt-oss-20b or Llama models for a four-block audit covering technical, narrative, macro and general risk. The position guardian monitors ticks and exits using a 4% leveraged hard stop, 6% leveraged take profit, breakeven after 1 ATR, 2x ATR trailing, 6-hour max hold and 1.5-hour stale exit. [PDF p.2]

2.3 Additional deductions from the trade log
The raw trade log reveals a deeper problem: several trades last 0–3 seconds even though the nominal strategy is described as 1-minute momentum. Examples include trades with 0s, 1s and 2s durations on LSK, VTHO, STEEM and CVC. This strongly suggests the live exit layer and/or execution simulation are interacting with intrabar price jumps in a way that makes the strategy effectively much faster than the candle timeframe implies. [PDF p.6] [PDF p.6]
A second warning is that the reported “0.8% unleveraged hard stop” is not actually a fixed 0.8% loss on every trade: the trade log contains several realized return figures larger than the nominal threshold, including -8.9%, -13.4% and other large leveraged-return values. That suggests gaps, tick execution, market orders, price selection, or a mismatch between intended stop logic and simulated fill logic. This should be treated as an execution-model bug until proven otherwise, not merely as a tuning issue. [PDF p.5] [PDF p.7]

The most important distinction is between signal quality and economic tradability. A model can predict direction slightly better than chance and still lose money if turnover is too high, the forecast horizon is mismatched to execution, or the expected move is smaller than spread+fees+slippage. A recent May 2026 walk-forward study on BTC explicitly found that naive sign-based ML strategies failed under a 10-basis-point transaction-cost assumption and recovered in selected cases only after applying a cost-aware threshold on forecast magnitude. [Research 2]
4. What current 2025–2026 research changes
4.1 Model choice: simpler models remain hard to beat
The strongest practical signal from current work is not “use the biggest model.” The 2026 BTC walk-forward comparison tested XGBoost, LSTM and iTransformer across 2018–2026 data and found XGBoost descriptively stronger than the neural alternatives, while emphasizing that cost-aware trade filtering mattered more than raw forecast direction. [Research 2]
That fits your situation well. Your existing feature set is tabular, heterogeneous and relatively small: technical indicators, volume and multi-timeframe state. A calibrated LightGBM/XGBoost classifier or probabilistic regressor is therefore a much more realistic first predictive layer than a large transformer. Current FreqAI tooling also supports live adaptive retraining and LightGBM deployment, making this practical for an individual developer. [Research 11–12]
4.2 Transformers and financial time-series foundation models
Financial time-series foundation models are worth testing, but they should be treated as optional research modules rather than automatic replacements. Kronos, published at AAAI 2026, is a finance-specific foundation model trained on more than 12 billion K-line records from 45 exchanges and reports strong zero-shot benchmark performance on ranking and volatility tasks. [Research 4]
However, other 2026 evidence is much more cautious: a study of foundation models in finance reports that off-the-shelf models can perform poorly in strict out-of-sample financial settings, while domain-specific pretraining is more promising. Another 2026 paper concludes that low signal-to-noise and structural breaks make finance fundamentally different from benchmark time series. [Research 13–14]
Recommendation: benchmark Kronos or another TSFM as a secondary forecasting model on 5m–30m data, but do not deploy it as the sole trade decision-maker until it beats a cost-aware XGBoost baseline under walk-forward validation.
4.3 LLMs: useful for narrative context, weak as tick-by-tick predictors
Your Groq LLM is currently being asked to approve already-generated momentum signals. The 100% approval rate shows that the model is not creating useful separation among candidates. This is a design problem, not proof that LLMs are useless. LLMs are much better suited to unstructured information—news, event interpretation, protocol announcements, social narrative shifts—than to estimating a 5-minute expected return from OHLCV alone.
An LLM should therefore become an asynchronous context/risk layer with a veto capability, not a mandatory approval step on every scalp. The quantitative model should first estimate expected return, downside, probability of hitting TP before SL, and expected cost. The LLM should only intervene when there is material text/event information or ambiguity.
4.4 Reinforcement learning: promising, but not the first fix
Recent work on risk-aware RL and futures-specific ensemble RL is interesting. FineFT (2025) reports risk reductions in a high-fidelity crypto-futures environment and emphasizes capability boundaries for regime changes. A separate 2025 study on PPO explicitly penalizes drawdown and turnover, but its out-of-sample benchmark still underperformed buy-and-hold on raw and risk-adjusted measures. [Research 5–6]
That is exactly why RL should be a later-stage project for your bot. You do not yet have a validated state representation, cost model, or stable reward definition. Training an RL agent before fixing those would likely teach the system to exploit simulator artifacts rather than a real edge.
5. Data and feature redesign
5.1 Add features that explain tradability, not just direction
Your current indicator set is reasonable but redundant: EMA, MACD and RSI all largely encode price trend/momentum. The missing information is market microstructure and cross-market context.
Order-book imbalance at several depths, spread, depth, microprice and short-horizon order-flow imbalance.
Trade aggressor imbalance, signed volume, trade intensity and liquidity shocks.
Funding rate, funding change and funding dispersion across major venues.
Open interest level and change, especially OI change conditional on price direction.
Liquidations and liquidation clusters as event/risk features rather than direct buy/sell signals.
BTC/ETH market state and beta of each altcoin to BTC/ETH; cross-sectional relative strength.
Realized volatility, ATR percentile, intraday volatility regime and volatility-of-volatility.
Exchange spread/depth and estimated impact for the actual planned order size.
Optional news/social sentiment branch with timestamp alignment and strict no-future-data rules.
2026 research on crypto market microstructure finds liquidity and price-discovery measures can carry predictive information, while 2026 work using BTC limit-order-book streams shows that LOB event data can improve short-horizon return forecasting beyond price series alone. [Research 7–8]
5.2 Do not simply add thousands of features
A 2025 crypto LOB study explicitly argues that better inputs and feature processing can matter more than adding another hidden layer. The practical implication is to build a compact set of interpretable, orthogonal features and measure incremental information gain, rather than feeding every indicator into a giant model. [Research 9]

7. Stop-loss, take-profit and position sizing redesign
Do not replace 0.8% with a single new fixed percentage. Stops must be structural and sized from volatility, then position size is derived from the chosen stop distance.
Use a 5m structural stop: below/above swing structure, with an ATR floor so the stop is not inside normal noise.
Use ATR multiples only as a guardrail, e.g. stop distance = max(structural distance, 1.5–2.5 ATR), then empirically optimize within a pre-registered grid.
Risk a fixed fraction of equity per trade, e.g. 0.25–0.50% during research. Leverage becomes an implementation variable, not a return target.
Prefer asymmetric exits: first reduce risk near +1R, then allow a runner when regime and trend strength support it.
Use a volatility-adjusted time stop: if price does not make sufficient progress within N bars, exit; do not keep a stale position indefinitely.
Hard global kill-switch: daily loss, rolling drawdown, exchange connectivity anomalies, spread explosion, or execution mismatch.
Your PDF proposes 1.5–2.5 ATR and reduced leverage, which is directionally sensible, but this should be validated across multiple market regimes rather than adopted blindly. [PDF p.4]
8. Churn controls and regime protection
This is one of the highest-impact redesigns because the audit shows the same few coins repeatedly generating losses. The system needs a “do not trade” state as a first-class output.
After two consecutive stop-outs on a symbol, block it for several hours and require a fresh regime transition before re-entry.
After three consecutive portfolio losses, cut risk by 50%; after a predefined daily loss, stop opening new positions.
Impose a minimum expected move / cost ratio, such as expected move at least 3x estimated round-trip friction for a market order. Test the threshold empirically.
Ban new trades when spread, depth or slippage estimates exceed normal percentiles.
Use a volatility percentile regime filter; high realized volatility is not automatically a signal to trade—sometimes it is a reason to trade smaller or not at all.
Cap trades per symbol per rolling window and require a meaningful new setup state before re-entry.
9. Execution model: the missing half of the strategy
Bitget states its standard futures fee is currently 0.02% maker and 0.06% taker, before VIP/BGB discounts. That makes execution choice economically material for a high-turnover bot. [Research 10]
Your current system effectively pays the taker rate repeatedly. Moving to maker orders can reduce friction, but “always use maker” is not the answer either: a non-filled order is an execution failure, and waiting for a fill can destroy the original signal. The execution engine should choose among marketable limit, passive post-only, and cancellation based on expected alpha decay, spread, queue/fill probability, and urgency.
Backtesting must include both fee and slippage assumptions. VectorBT exposes explicit fees and slippage inputs, while Freqtrade’s current backtesting stack includes fee accounting and lookahead-analysis tooling. [Research 11]

13. Top five improvements most likely to matter
1. Stop trading marginal setups
Add an explicit net-edge gate: no trade unless predicted move comfortably exceeds fees + spread + slippage + a risk buffer. This is the single biggest conceptual upgrade.
2. Kill churn and concentration
Cooldowns, loss breakers, symbol re-entry limits and a daily loss cap should stop LSK/CVC-style feedback loops.
3. Rebuild the stop/size/execution model
Use structural + ATR stops, fixed fractional risk, realistic fills, and execution-aware order types.
4. Make ML a calibrated selective predictor
Start with XGBoost/LightGBM on 5m/15m multi-source features; output probability, expected move and uncertainty—not just direction.
5. Establish institutional-grade validation
Walk-forward + purged/embargoed CV + realistic costs + DSR/PBO-style selection control + weeks of paper/live shadow testing before scaling.
14. What I would not do yet
Do not add more LLM calls or more API keys as a substitute for a real statistical edge.
Do not optimize RSI/EMA/ATR thresholds against the 24-hour result; that is classic parameter overfitting.
Do not jump directly to reinforcement learning.
Do not assume a higher model accuracy or win rate automatically means higher net PnL.
Do not switch every order to maker just to lower fees; fill quality and signal decay must be modeled.
Do not interpret one profitable day or one profitable coin as evidence of a durable strategy.
15. Practical target state
A robust version of this system should spend most of its time doing nothing. A good intraday system can continuously scan hundreds of contracts but take only a small number of trades when the expected net edge, market regime, liquidity, execution quality and risk constraints align. The desired output at every decision point is not simply “LONG” or “SHORT”; it is a structured record such as: regime=TREND_UP, candidate=YES, P(up)=0.64, expected_move=0.92%, expected_cost=0.22%, stop=0.58%, target=1.25%, edge_score=PASS, risk=0.35%, execution=LIMIT, confidence=HIGH.
That makes the system auditable. Every trade can later be decomposed into whether the prediction was wrong, the regime classification was wrong, the cost model was wrong, the execution was poor, or the risk policy was wrong. Your current system does not yet provide that clean attribution, which is why the right next step is an evidence pipeline rather than another AI model.

17. Bottom line
The existing bot is close to a useful research platform, but it should not be treated as a profitable trading strategy yet. The audit shows the immediate bottleneck is economic selectivity: the bot trades too often, trades instruments with noisy microstructure, pays too much friction, and treats an LLM as a universal approval stamp. The highest-probability path is to make the system deliberately boring—fewer trades, better liquidity, stronger statistical filters, explicit cost thresholds, risk-based sizing, realistic execution and rigorous walk-forward validation—then layer more advanced AI on top only when it adds measurable incremental edge.
